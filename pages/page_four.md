## Digital systems I depend on every day

It is easy to think of digital technologies and apps on my phone as tools that simply make life more convenient. However, when I started reflecting on the systems I use every day, I realized that many of them are much more than optional tools. They are a necessity which allows me to participate in society, communicate with others, travel, work, and manage everyday tasks. Some of these technologies are services I actively chose because I enjoy them, while others are effectively mandatory because Danish society, my employer, or financial institutions require them. In this paper, I explore ten digital systems I use on a normal day, rank them according to their importance, examine three systems in greater detail, and reflect on what these technologies reveal about dependency and control in digital society.

The point of this assignment is to realize that we take digital technologies often for granted, and their functioning became almost invisible in everyday life. Most people do not spend much time thinking about the systems they use because they are so integrated into their daily routines. We tend to notice them only when something stops working, such as losing internet access, forgetting a password, or being unable to log into an important service. Looking closely at these technologies reveals how dependent modern life has become on digital infrastructure. It also raises questions about who controls these systems, who benefits from them, and how much influence users actually have over technologies that are essential for participating in society.

### Digital technologies I use every day

From the moment I wake up until I go to bed, I interact with numerous digital systems. I start my day by checking notifications on my phone and choosing which ones to open first. Usually, I open messages on WhatsApp first, then move on to checking Gmail for messages from my university, work, and other services. During my commute, I use the Rejsekort app to travel by public transport. If I buy coffee or groceries, I always pay using Apple wallet, which has replaced physical payment cards for me here, in Denmark. At work, I use Planday to check my shifts and schedule changes. Throughout the day I may need MitID to verify my identity when logging into public services or banking platforms. I also receive official communications through e-Boks, which is the standard channel for communication with Danish authorities. I manage my finances through Lunar banking app, listen to music or podcasts on Spotify, and complete language exercises on Duolingo before going to sleep. 

These technologies are connected to almost every aspect of my life. Some help me communicate with other people, while others allow me to travel, work, learn, or manage my finances. Individually, each app seems relatively simple, but together they support my daily routines. If several of them failed at the same time, it would become difficult to go on with my day as usual. I also noticed that many of these technologies depend on one another. For example, Gmail is often used to recover passwords for other services. MitID is required to access online banking, while Apple Wallet depends on bank cards and digital payment infrastructure. Similarly, WhatsApp relies on smartphones, internet access, and contact information stored digitally. This means that losing access to one system can sometimes create problems across multiple areas of life. The technologies may appear separate, but in reality, they are deeply interconnected.

When looking at my list, I was surprised by how few technologies are used purely for entertainment. Although Spotify and Duolingo are optional, most of the systems I use serve practical purposes and are tied to other responsibilities, such as work, transportation, communication, or administration. This can illustrate how digital technologies have evolved from convenient tools into essential parts of everyday life. 

Out of these ten technologies, only three were entirely my own choice: WhatsApp, Spotify, and Duolingo. I use them because I find them useful and enjoyable. The remaining systems have largely been imposed on me by institutions. MitID and e-Boks are essential for interacting with Danish public authorities, Planday is required by my employer, Rejsekort is necessary for commuting, Apple Wallet has become my primary payment solution, and Lunar functions as my every day bank account. Gmail may technically be optional, but in practice many educational institutions, employers, and online services expect people to have an email account. This distinction between chosen and imposed technologies became more apparent as I noticed how dependent I am on systems that I never consciously selected for myself.

### The Ranking

When I ranked these technologies, I did not focus on which ones I enjoy the most. Instead, I considered how much my life would be disrupted if each suddenly stopped working one day.

1. The most important system for me is **MitID**. In Denmark, it functions as a digital key to a large number of public and private services. Without MitID, I would struggle to access my digital identification, online banking, public administration platforms, but also online libraries and other services. Losing access would most definitely have the biggest effect on my life in Denmark.

2. The second most important system is **e-Boks**. This is where I receive official communication from public institutions, healthcare information, and other mail. Missing access to e-Boks would mean potentially missing important information about my bills, healthcare, taxes, or education.

3. Third is **Apple Wallet**. I rarely carry a physical payment card because I almost always pay with my phone. If Appe Wallet stopped working, I would lose my primary method of payment. While alternatives exist, everyday purchases would immediately become more inconvenient for me.

4. I ranked **Planday** fourth because it is essential for my job. If this app disappeared, I would no longer have easy access to my shifts and working hours. Communication between employees and managers would also become significantly harder.

5. The fifth most important technology is **Rejsekort**. As someone who lives far from the city center and university, and relies on public transportation, losing access would make commuting and travelling much more complicated. I could still purchase tickets manually, but the process would be less convenient and more time-consuming.

6. **WhatsApp** comes sixth. Although it is not required by any institution, it is still one of the most important social technologies in my life. Most of my communication with friends and family happens these. If WhatsApp disappeared, I would eventually reconnect with most people through other platforms, but communication would become fragmented.

7. **Gmail** ranks seventh because it functions as much more than an email service. It is connected to many online accounts and serves also as a recovery method when passwords are forgotten. Losing Gmail could therefore also mean losing access to numerous other digital services.

8. I placed **Spotify** eighth because it helps me to relax while I listen to music or some podcasts. I could still function pretty well if Spotify stopped working, but it would make my day a bit worse.

9. **Lunar** ranks ninth because although I need banking services, I don’t actually need to access it every day, and there are other ways how to communicate with my bank.

10. Finally, **Duolingo** comes tenth. It is useful for learning languages, and yes, losing my streak would hurt, but out of all the other apps, this one is the least necessary for every day life.


### Examining three digital systems

- **WhatsApp** was created by Jan Koum and Brian Acton and was later acquired by Meta in 2014. (Blake, 2026) The platform was designed to provide a simple and affordable way for people to communicate through internet-based messaging, rather than SMS.
The application operates globally and is owned by Meta, which is based in the United States. (2026) However, users are also subject to local regulations, which means that both Meta’s policies and European data protection laws influence how the service operates.
The platform was originally funded by its founders and outside investors before becoming part of Meta’s portfolio. Although WhatsApp is free to use, it remains connected to a large corporate ecosystem and depends on Meta’s financial resources and infrastructure for its operation. (2026)
- **MitID** was developed as Denmark’s new electronic identification (eID) solution, replacing NemID. (Kingo and Aranha, 2023) The system was created through cooperation between the Danish public sector and the financial sector, with the aim of providing secure digital identification for citizens and residents of Denmark.
Unlike WhatsApp, MitID is closely linked to Danish society and operates primarily within Danish and European legal frameworks. Because it is used for authentication across both public and private services, it could be described as a critical part of Danish digital infrastructure. (2023) Its role extends far beyond simple login functionality, as many everyday services depend on it.
Funding was shared between public authorities and Danish financial institutions, which fits the fact that both sectors benefit from a common national identification system. Kingo and Aranha (2023) also note that the importance of MitID makes security particularly important, since weaknesses in the system could affect access to a large number of essential services.
- **Planday** was founded in Denmark to help businesses manage employee scheduling, shift planning, and workforce communication (Tracxn, 2026). The company was created as a commercial solution for organisations that depend on shift work. Although it originated in Denmark, it now functions also internationally. 
According to Tracxn (2026), Planday has received funding from several investors and operates as a venture-backed company. Today, the platform generates revenue through subscriptions paid by employers that use the software to organize their work. (2026)


### Reflection and conclusion

The most surprising part of this assignment was realizing how many digital systems I rely on without actively choosing them. Before making my list, I assumed that most technologies in my life were personal preferences. But I discovered that many are connected to institutions. 
The most difficult question to answer was probably who paid for the technology. Information about ownership is usually easy to find, but funding is often less visible. Yet these motivations can reveal a great deal about why a technology was created and whose interests it serves.
Overall, this assignment showed that digital technologies are much more that convenient tools. They shape how people communicate, work, travel, identify themselves, and participate in society in general. Understanding who designed them and who funds them provides a more critical perspective on the technologies that have become essential for everyday life.
